Overview · what this is

A bathing practice, kept weekly

Eight movements, in a fixed order, held on Sunday evenings. The sequence is borrowed from bathing cultures that have thought about this for a long time, and each borrowing is named. Where a claim has evidence behind it, the evidence is cited and graded. Where a practice is inherited rather than tested, it is marked as inherited.

The sequence read as a temperature score. The shape — rise, peak, sharp drop, settle — is the part that matters; the numbers are a starting position, not a prescription.
The organising principle

Across the traditions gathered here, the bath ends when the sequence ends. It does not end when the bather judges themselves finished. Japanese public bathing separates washing from soaking so completely that the two happen in different places, with the tub reserved for water that is already clean. Roman bathing moved through rooms in order. Jewish immersion and Islamic ghusl define completion by a form correctly carried out.

This resource adopts that structure directly: completion is defined by the sequence, not by sensation. When the eighth movement is done, the bath is done.

A note on the medium

Water is the other body here. It has been rained, run, pooled, frozen, evaporated and returned, and it holds nothing it has passed through permanently. It is a substance defined by transit rather than accumulation. That is its entire relevance, and it is not offered as a metaphor to think with but as a material fact to sit in.

The map · where this comes from

The Atlas

Each movement in the sequence is borrowed. This page names the source, describes the practice in its own terms rather than mine, and marks the difference between what I have read about and what I have adapted.

The warm room, and its domestic descendant. Every tradition on this page arrived independently at the same idea — that warmth should come before work.

Traditions drawn on
Japan · sentō, onsen, furo
Washing before entering
Bathers wash and rinse completely at a seated tap station before entering the tub. The bath water is shared and stays clean; the tub is for soaking, not cleaning. Seasonal baths mark the calendar — yuzu-yu at the winter solstice, shōbu-yu with sweet flag leaves on 5 May.
Contributes: movements 3, 4, 5 — and the entire premise that washing and soaking are different acts.
Rome · thermae
Graded rooms in order
Bathing progressed through rooms of ascending and then descending temperature — apodyterium, tepidarium, caldarium, frigidarium — with oiling and strigil work as part of the sequence rather than an addition to it.
Contributes: the thermal arc itself, and the idea of the bath as a composed progression.
Ottoman & North African · hammam
Warmth before work
Extended time in a warm, humid room precedes any cleansing, so that the skin is softened before it is touched. Olive-oil soap and long social duration are characteristic.
Contributes: movement 2 — warming as a stage in its own right, not a preamble.
Finland · sauna
Heat, then cold, then rest
Alternation between heat and cold, closed by a rest period that is treated as part of the practice rather than its aftermath. Finnish sauna culture was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2020.
Contributes: movement 6, and the principle that the rest afterward is inside the ritual.
South Asia · abhyanga
Oil, applied slowly
Warm oil self-massage, traditionally preceding rather than following bathing, applied with sustained rather than brisk contact.
Contributes: movement 7. I have inverted the order — oil after water, not before — because that timing has dermatological support and the traditional order does not, for my skin.
Jewish and Islamic law
Completion as a form
Mikveh immersion and ghusl are religious obligations with defined conditions for validity. Their completion is established by the act being performed correctly, not by how the person feels afterward.
Contributes: the structural principle only. I am borrowing a form of reasoning, not a rite, and this is not a practice of mine.
What is not mine to instruct

I live on the unceded territories of the Musqueam, Squamish and Tsleil-Waututh Nations. Bathing and cedar-brushing practices are living, ongoing traditions here, held by the people whose they are. They are not a technique to be extracted into a document like this one.

I have left them out of the sequence deliberately. If I ever come to any part of that, it will be through relationship and invitation, on terms set by the people offering it, and not from reading. Naming the absence is more accurate than filling it.

On borrowing

Every element above is used at a remove from its context. A sentō is a public building and a social institution; my bathroom is neither. What I am adopting is a structure and a set of proportions, with the source stated. I remain a student of these traditions rather than a practitioner of them, and this document should be read as one person's assembly, attributed.

The practice · head to toe

The Sequence

Total duration roughly 75 minutes, begun about two hours before intended sleep. Order is fixed. Duration and temperature are adjustable. The one rule that does not move is that no cleaning happens after movement 4.

01Thresholdafter the Roman apodyterium — the undressing room
Temperature
Ambient
Duration
10 minutes
Function
Context

The room is prepared before I am. Overhead light off, low warm light on. Phone out of the room. Towels, robe, shea and a glass of water set out so that nothing has to be fetched later. The herb infusion is brewed at this point and the tub begun.

The scent is set here and nowhere else. A single seasonal scent, used only during this ritual, so that it becomes specific to it. Citrus peel simmering, or a few drops of oil on the shower floor away from the water stream, or hinoki flakes waiting in the tub.

Citrus stays in the air, not on skin. Bergamot, expressed lemon, lime and bitter orange contain furocoumarins and are phototoxic on skin under subsequent UV exposure. The airborne version carries the whole association without the risk.

02Warmingafter the tepidarium and the hammam warm room
Temperature
36–38 °C
Duration
8 minutes
Function
Soften, unhurry

Under the rain shower, doing nothing. No soap, no cloth, no task. This is the stage most often skipped and the one that makes the difference between a wash and a bath.

Feet first: I stand and let the water reach the feet and ankles before anything else, then let it move up. The hair goes under here and is simply wetted. Warm water raises stratum corneum hydration and pliability, which is why every tradition that scrubs, scrubs late.

03Washingkarada wo arau — seated, outside the tub
Temperature
36–38 °C
Duration
6 minutes, timed
Function
Surfactant, minimally

Six minutes, and the order is head to toe, always the same:

iScalpOnly on hair-wash Sundays. See Particular Waters.
iiFaceWater temperature stepped down. Separate protocol, separate product.
iiiHands and forearmsThe only place I use soap generously and without hesitation.
ivBodyDiluted castile soap at four sites only: underarms, groin, and anywhere actually soiled. Everywhere else, warm water and a soft cotton cloth. No abrasive scrubber.
vFeetLast, and the one place I use a brush. Between the toes deliberately.
Why so little soap

Healthy skin surface pH sits below 5. True saponified soap, including castile, is alkaline at roughly pH 9–10, and repeated alkaline washing raises skin pH and disrupts barrier lipids. Barrier disruption produces tightness, roughness and itch — sensations that are easily read as unclean, prompting further washing.

Metro Vancouver's water is very soft. Soft water plus true soap leaves a slick residue that many people experience as incomplete rinsing. Diluting the soap roughly 1:3 in a foamer and restricting it to the four sites addresses both problems at once.

04The Rinsekake-yu — the pouring-over
Temperature
34–36 °C
Duration
2 minutes
Function
The pivot

Standing, water only, from crown to feet, without interruption. Nothing is scrubbed, checked or returned to.

This is the structural centre of the whole sequence. Cleaning ends here by declaration, not by assessment. Everything after this point happens to a body that has been established as clean. If a doubt arrives during the soak, it is answered by the fact that movement 4 was completed, not by getting out.

05The Soakfuro — clean water, no work
Temperature
38–40 °C
Duration
12–20 minutes
Function
Heat, herb, stillness

The herb infusion has been poured in and the cloth bag floats. Nothing is done in the tub. No washing, no phone, no reading on the first Sunday of a month.

The infusion method matters more than the herbs. A dry bag dropped into a full tub barely extracts. I brew four litres on the stove, covered, off the boil, for 25 minutes, strain it into the tub, then float the spent bag as well. Bag: unbleached cotton muslin, drawstring, about 20 × 25 cm, filled with 1½ cups dried herb.

Timing is set by sleep, not by preference. Passive body heating in warm water roughly one to two hours before bed is the interval with the best supporting evidence for shortening sleep onset. Sunday's bath is scheduled backwards from Monday's alarm.

Heat, with my medication

I take a medication associated with heat intolerance. I have not found bathing at these temperatures to affect me, and the range above sits close to body temperature rather than well above it. If that ever changes, the adjustment is to lower the soak to 37–38 °C and shorten it, rather than to remove the movement.

06Coolingafter the frigidarium and Nordic alternation
Temperature
Ambient or cooler
Duration
1–3 minutes
Function
Descent

Out of the tub, wrapped, onto the balcony. Downtown air at night, standing still, not looking at anything in particular.

Balcony conditions govern this movement more than temperature does. High-rise wind and overlooking sightlines both determine whether standing still outside is actually restful, so the practical variables are a screen or planter at the rail, a heavy robe, and a corner out of the prevailing wind. In February this movement is 45 seconds. In July it is the longest part of the evening.

If the balcony is unusable, the substitute is an open window and a cool cloth at the back of the neck and the wrists. The function is a clear thermal downward step, not the outdoors specifically.

07Anointingafter abhyanga, timed to damp skin
Temperature
Room
Duration
6 minutes
Function
Seal, and be touched

Patted, not rubbed, until just damp. Shea warmed between the palms until it turns liquid. Applied while the skin is still damp — emollient onto damp skin is standard dermatological practice for compromised barrier function.

The stroke has a speed. C-tactile afferents, the unmyelinated fibres associated with the affective quality of touch, respond most strongly to slow stroking in the region of 1–10 cm per second. That is much slower than an ordinary moisturising motion. In practice: one long stroke down the outside of a thigh should take about three seconds.

Order reverses the wash — feet first, upward, face last. Plain shea whipped with a spoonful of jojoba or camellia oil spreads without dragging and loses the graininess.

08The Closingmine
Temperature
—
Duration
1 minute
Function
The end

Three actions, always the same three, always in this order: drain the tub, drink the glass of water, turn off the light. Then a sentence said aloud — the same one each week, chosen by me, changed only at the solstice.

The closing exists because a ritual with no ending has no way of being finished. This movement is the answer to the question of whether the bath is over. It is over.

The practice · by region

Particular Waters

Five parts of the body that do not follow the general protocol, each with its own frequency, temperature and material. Frequency is the variable that does the most work in every one of them.

Hair · long, red, wavy

Frequency: shampoo on the first and third Sunday. Water-only rinse and conditioner on the others.

Wavy and curly hair transports sebum down the shaft less efficiently than straight hair, so the mid-lengths and ends run drier while the scalp does not. This is the mechanical reason that reduced shampoo frequency suits this hair type, and it is a better-founded reason than most of what is claimed about hair.

  • Pre-wash oiling. Coconut oil applied to the lengths before washing reduces protein loss from hair, in both undamaged and chemically treated hair, in a way that mineral and sunflower oils do not. It is the single best-evidenced thing on this page. Twenty minutes before movement 2, mid-lengths and ends only, not the scalp.
  • Shampoo the scalp, not the hair. Surfactant at the roots; the lengths are cleaned adequately by what runs down.
  • Not castile soap. Alkaline soap raises the cuticle and, with long hair, increases friction and tangling. This is one place where Dr. Bronner's is the wrong tool despite being the right ethic.
  • Detangle wet, with conditioner in, from the ends upward. Wet hair is at its most vulnerable to mechanical damage; combing from the ends up removes the force that breaks it.
  • Cool final rinse, then a cotton t-shirt rather than terry. Terry loops catch and roughen the cuticle.

Seasonal note: a rosemary or nettle infusion as a final rinse is a traditional practice with limited clinical evidence. I use it because I like it, and I record it as inherited rather than established.

Face · sensitive, fair

Frequency: once during the ritual, at a lower water temperature than the body.

  • Temperature down, not up. Hot water plus surfactant is the main irritant combination for a sensitive face. Lukewarm, and briefly.
  • A syndet or cream cleanser, not soap. A pH-balanced synthetic detergent cleanser near skin pH disturbs the barrier measurably less than alkaline soap. This is the one place I would not substitute castile.
  • No exfoliant, no brush, no cloth. Hands only.
  • Shea last and sparingly on the face, or a lighter oil if shea sits heavily. Applied damp, like the body.
  • Steam, if wanted, belongs in movement 2 — over the herb infusion with a towel, three minutes, before any cleansing.
Hands

Frequency: the exception. Hands are washed as often as they need to be, and the restraint that governs the rest of the body does not apply here.

Within the ritual, hands get the full soap treatment at movement 3 and a deliberate slow massage at movement 7 — palm, each finger drawn out from base to tip, the web between thumb and index, the wrist. This is the one part of the body I can reach comfortably with both hands, which makes it the best site for the slow-stroke speed described in movement 7.

Hand skin is the most frequently barrier-disrupted skin on a body. Shea at the bedside as well as in the bathroom is a reasonable redundancy.

Feet

Frequency: every Sunday, and this is the part of the ritual I would keep if I could keep only one.

Warming the feet promotes vasodilation in the extremities, and the degree of distal-to-proximal skin temperature gradient is a strong physiological predictor of how quickly sleep begins. A footbath is therefore not a decorative addition to a sleep ritual; it is one of the more direct levers in it.

  • A separate footbath, before the tub, on weeks when there is no time for the full sequence. Basin, 38–40 °C, ten minutes, a handful of the same herbs. This is the abbreviated ritual — see The Turning Year.
  • Feet are washed last and are the only site with a brush.
  • Feet are oiled first at movement 7, and are given more time than anywhere else.
  • Socks after oiling. Cotton, loose. Occlusion increases emollient effect and keeps the feet warm into sleep.
The menstruating body

Frequency: the sequence continues unchanged. Three modifications.

  • Warm water only on the vulva. Nothing internal, ever. The vagina is self-cleaning and maintains its own acidic flora. Douching is associated with bacterial vaginosis and other adverse outcomes and is recommended against. Soap on the vulva is unnecessary and, on sensitive skin, counterproductive. Warm water and a hand.
  • Local heat is the intervention with evidence. Continuous low-level topical heat over the lower abdomen has been shown to relieve primary dysmenorrhoea, in at least one trial performing comparably to ibuprofen. During the soak, this is a folded facecloth wrung out hot and laid on the lower belly; after, a covered hot water bottle held there through movement 8 and into sleep.
  • The soak may be shortened and cooled to 37–38 °C if heat feels wrong on any given month. Herbal additions are not required. If any are used, ginger or chamomile in the tub are both benign topically; I do not make internal claims for either.

The reason the sequence is unchanged is deliberate. The structure of the ritual treats a menstruating body as a body that bathes, without a separate category, and the modifications above are practical rather than purificatory. Traditions that treat menstruation as a state requiring ritual cleansing exist and are meaningful within their own religious frameworks; that is not the framework this document operates in, and importing the vocabulary without the framework would be inaccurate.

The calendar · fifty-two Sundays

The Turning Year

The sequence does not change. What changes is the guest: one tradition emphasised per Sunday of the month, one scent per season, and two fixed dates the year turns on. The spine stays; the face rotates.

The four Sundays

One emphasis per week of the month, rotating. Everything else in the sequence is held constant, so each Sunday is a single-variable change.

SundayEmphasisWhat changes
FirstJapaneseSilence. No reading, no music, no talking. The soak is the longest of the month and entirely unoccupied. Hinoki in the water.
SecondNordicMovement 6 doubles. Two cooling steps rather than one, returning to the tub between them. Shortest soak, sharpest contrast.
ThirdHammamMovement 2 doubles. Long warming, steam over the infusion, olive-oil soap. Hair wash falls here.
FourthRomanComposed. I write the temperature score in advance on paper — five or six numbered steps — and follow only what I wrote. The bath is a piece I set for myself the week before.
The fifth Sunday

Four or five times a year a month has a fifth Sunday. That one has no protocol at all. Nothing is prescribed, nothing recorded, no score written. It exists so that the system contains its own exception rather than being broken by one.

Four scents, four seasons

The scent is set at movement 1 and used nowhere else in my week. Each is chosen for what I can source at an apothecary or grow in a container, not for what a boutique carries.

Two fixed dates
iWinter solstice · yuzu-yuWhole yuzu floated in the bath on the longest night, following the Japanese tōji custom. This is the date the closing sentence changes. If the potted yuzu ever fruits, the fruit comes from the balcony.
ii5 May · shōbu-yuSweet flag leaves in the bath, following the Japanese custom for that date. Sourced, not grown. The one bath of the year that is green all the way through.
When there is not enough capacity

The ritual has a defined short form, so that a low week is a shorter bath rather than a missed one. The short form is movements 1, 4, 7 and 8 — threshold, rinse, anointing, closing — with a footbath in place of the tub. Twenty minutes. It still counts, and it counts fully, because the sequence was completed.

Herbs for the tub · non-staining

Sourced in bulk from an herbal dispensary rather than pre-packaged. Roughly 1½ cups dried per bath, decocted then strained in.

  • Colloidal oatmeal or oat straw — the best-evidenced item in the list. Colloidal oatmeal is a recognised over-the-counter skin protectant, with anti-inflammatory and anti-itch activity described in the dermatological literature. This is the base of every infusion I make.
  • Marshmallow root — mucilaginous; changes the hand-feel of the water measurably. Cold-infuse this one separately, as heat degrades the mucilage.
  • German chamomile — mild, traditional, minimal staining.
  • Linden flower, lemon balm, lavender buds — aromatic, non-staining, all growable or cheap in bulk.
  • Pale rose petals — deep red cultivars can tint a tub; pale ones do not.
Excluded, and why
  • Calendula, turmeric, alkanet, madder — staining.
  • St John's wort — stains red, is photosensitising, and has extensive drug-interaction potential. Excluded on all three counts.
  • Neat essential oils in bathwater — they do not disperse and contact skin undiluted at the surface. If used at all, they go into the air at movement 1 or into a carrier oil, never loose in the tub.
  • Expressed citrus oils on skin — furocoumarin phototoxicity.
  • Abrasive mitts, gommage, salt scrubs, dry brushing — mechanically at odds with the aim.
  • Immunomodulating herbs taken on my own initiative. Topical exposure is low, but echinacea, astragalus and similar carry reputations I am not qualified to weigh against an autoimmune condition. These are a question for the herbalist at the counter and for my physician, not a decision I make from a shelf.
Honest pricing
  • Epsom salt — the transdermal magnesium absorption claim is not supported by adequate evidence; a 2017 review concluded the data were insufficient. The warm water is doing the work. Buy the cheapest agricultural-grade bag, or skip it. Do not buy a branded version.
  • Hinoki bath flakes — wood shavings and water, no leave-on chemistry, around $15 at Old Faithful. Priced fairly for what it is and does something no herb does.
  • Bulk herbs from an apothecary — the correct place to spend. Buying loose, by weight, from people who can answer a question is both cheaper and better than anything pre-blended.
  • Anything marketed as a "ritual", "detox" or "cleanse" — no function assignable to a movement. Excluded by the rule at the top of this page.
Materials · made and grown

What I Grow

Downtown Vancouver, containers, a balcony. Winter cold is not the limiting factor here; wind and light are. This page is the long part of the practice — the part measured in years rather than Sundays.

Containers, this year
  • Lemon balm — vigorous, container-suited, cuts repeatedly through the season. Dries well.
  • Chamomile — direct-sown, generous, wants sun and shelter from wind.
  • Lavender — needs the sunniest, driest, windiest corner and sharp drainage. The one plant that likes a balcony better than a garden.
  • Calendula — grown for the plant, not for the tub. It stains.
  • Rose — a pale, scented, container-appropriate variety. Petals harvested in the morning after dew has lifted.
  • Rosemary — evergreen, survives a coastal winter outdoors in a pot, useful for the hair rinse.
The long project
A potted yuzu

Citrus junos is the most cold-hardy of the true citrus. Coastal Vancouver's winters are mild and downtown is milder still; the real constraints on a balcony are wind exposure, winter light and root-zone temperature in a container, all of which are addressable with a sheltered corner, a large pot, and wrapping in a cold snap.

Yuzu fruits in early winter. The Japanese yuzu-yu custom places whole fruit in the bath at the solstice. If the tree ever fruits, the December bath is made from the balcony, on the shortest night, joining a childhood association with citrus in winter to a bathing custom that arrived at the same conclusion independently.

Realistically this is a multi-year proposition and may not fruit. The tree is worth the balcony space regardless — the leaves are aromatic, and a bay leaf's worth of yuzu leaf in the tub is available long before any fruit is.

Made rather than bought
  • The muslin bags — straight seams, drawstring channel, an hour for six.
  • The whipped shea — shea, a carrier oil, a hand mixer, a jar. Nothing else.
  • Dried herbs — screens or paper bags, out of sun, stored in jars with the harvest date on them.
  • Cold-process soap — the natural next step, and the one that requires proper instruction before starting. Lye handling is not a thing to learn from a website. The apothecary near me runs workshops; that is the route.
  • The closing sentence — written once, kept in pencil, changed at the solstice.
Reference · what is actually known

What Is Known

Grading follows the convention used elsewhere in this repository. A weak grade is not a reason to remove a practice; it is a reason to describe it accurately and to hold it as inherited rather than demonstrated.

StrongWarm bathing before bed
A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis found that passive body heating in water at roughly 40–43 °C, one to two hours before bedtime, shortened sleep onset latency and improved subjective sleep quality. This is the finding that sets the timing of movement 5.
StrongWarm feet and sleep onset
Distal skin warming and the resulting vasodilation are closely associated with the speed of sleep onset; degree of distal vasodilation was the best physiological predictor of sleep onset in a widely cited 1999 study. This is why the footbath is the element retained in the short form.
StrongSkin pH and cleanser alkalinity
Skin surface pH is on average below 5. Alkaline cleansers raise it and disrupt barrier lipids; syndet cleansers formulated near skin pH do so measurably less. Well established in dermatological literature.
StrongEmollient onto damp skin
Moisturiser is a first-line component of care for compromised skin barriers in published clinical guidelines. Application to damp skin is standard clinical practice, though the specific "within three minutes" figure is convention rather than a trial result.
StrongCoconut oil and hair protein loss
Coconut oil applied pre- or post-wash reduced protein loss in undamaged, bleached, UV-exposed and chemically treated hair; mineral and sunflower oils did not. Attributed to lauric acid's affinity for hair protein and its ability to penetrate the shaft.
StrongColloidal oatmeal
Recognised as an over-the-counter skin protectant by the US Food and Drug Administration, with anti-inflammatory and anti-pruritic activity characterised in the literature.
StrongCitrus phototoxicity
Furocoumarins, chiefly bergapten, in bergamot and expressed citrus peel oils produce phototoxic reactions on UV exposure. Documented in case reports and reflected in international fragrance restrictions.
StrongAgainst douching
Vaginal douching is associated with bacterial vaginosis, pelvic inflammatory disease and other adverse outcomes, and is recommended against. The vagina maintains its own flora.
ModerateC-tactile afferents and stroke speed
Unmyelinated CT afferents in hairy skin respond most vigorously to slow stroking at approximately 1–10 cm/s, and firing rate correlates with rated pleasantness. The neurophysiology is solid; the leap from that to a therapeutic effect of self-applied slow touch is mine and is not established.
ModerateLocal heat for dysmenorrhoea
Continuous low-level topical heat relieved primary dysmenorrhoea in a randomised trial, performing comparably to ibuprofen. Replication is limited but the mechanism is plausible and the intervention is very low risk.
ModerateSauna bathing and mortality
A large Finnish prospective cohort found frequency-dependent inverse associations between sauna bathing and cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. Observational; causation is not established, the cohort was middle-aged Finnish men, and a home bath is not a sauna.
MixedBalneotherapy
Reviews of bathing-based therapy for conditions including fibromyalgia report benefit, but the literature suffers from small samples, weak blinding and heterogeneous protocols. Enough to justify the practice; not enough to make claims from.
MixedCold exposure and mood
A large randomised trial found routine cold showering reduced self-reported sickness absence. Claims specifically about cold water and depression trace substantially to a 2008 paper in a journal of hypotheses, which is a proposal rather than evidence. Movement 6 is kept for the structure of the descent, not for a mood claim.
WeakTransdermal magnesium
A 2017 review found the evidence for meaningful magnesium absorption through skin insufficient. Epsom salt stays only as a texture preference.
WeakAromatherapy for anxiety
Inhaled lavender and similar have small, heterogeneous and largely unblinded supporting trials. The scent in movement 1 is used as a deliberately conditioned contextual cue, which is a different and better-supported mechanism than a pharmacological one.
InheritedHerbal bath additions generally
Chamomile, linden, lemon balm, rose, rosemary rinses. Long traditional use, minimal controlled evidence for bath application specifically. Retained as inherited practice, recorded as such, and not claimed to treat anything.
FrameworkCompletion by sequence
The organising principle of this document — that the bath ends when the form ends — is a structural observation drawn from ritual traditions, not a tested intervention. It is the most important idea here and the least evidenced. Named plainly so it is not mistaken for a finding.

Reference · scope

Limits

What this document is for, and the points at which it hands off.

What this is not

Not treatment, not diagnosis, and not a therapeutic protocol for any condition. It is a personal practice with its reasoning shown. Nothing here treats an autoimmune condition, depression or anxiety, and no claim in this document should be read as saying otherwise.

Questions that belong to other people
  • Any herb taken internally, and any herb at all given my condition and medication. The herbalist at the counter for the plant, my physician or pharmacist for the interaction. Not this document, and not a shelf.
  • Heat tolerance if my response to it changes.
  • Photosensitivity — whether anything I take carries it, confirmed rather than assumed.
  • Any new or changing skin finding. Fair skin at this latitude warrants an actual examination, not a bathing routine.
On the sensation this practice is built around

This ritual is designed so that completion is established by finishing the sequence rather than by arriving at a feeling of being clean. That design choice is deliberate and it is the reason for the fixed order and the closing.

If the sense of not being clean intensifies, extends past the ritual, produces washing outside it, or takes up more time or distress than I want it to, that is information about something the structure of a bath is not built to resolve, and it is worth raising with a clinician. Contamination-related distress is well characterised and responds to specific treatment. Noticing that would not mean the practice failed; it would mean the practice was doing its job by making the pattern visible.

On accuracy

Citations in this document should be verified against the sources before being relied on or repeated elsewhere. Where I could not source a claim, I have marked the practice as inherited rather than filling the gap. That marking is load-bearing, and future edits should preserve it.
